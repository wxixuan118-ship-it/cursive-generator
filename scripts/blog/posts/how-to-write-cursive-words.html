Most people who struggle with cursive words do not have a letter problem. They can make a decent a, a clean l and a readable g. What goes wrong is the space between letters: a join that dips too low, a pen lift in the middle of a word, an o that swallows the letter after it. Cursive is a joined hand, so the connection strokes carry as much of the shape as the letters themselves.

This guide covers exactly that part. You will learn the four kinds of join, the handful of letters that behave differently, how to space and slant your cursive words so a line reads evenly, and how to build from two-letter pairs up to full sentences. If any single letter shape is still shaky, check it against the cursive letters A–Z reference before you start joining.

Why cursive words are harder than cursive letters

A letter written alone starts and stops wherever it likes. Inside a word, every letter has to begin where the previous one finished. That changes things. The undercurve that starts an i when you write it alone becomes the tail of whatever came before it. The loop of an e has to grow out of the exit stroke of a b, which leaves from a completely different height.

So the practical question behind "how to write a word in cursive" is really: where does each letter end, and where does the next one need to start? Letters end in one of two places, down at the baseline or up at the midline. Letters begin in a few predictable ways too. Match the two and you have a join. Once you know the four patterns below, almost every word you will ever write is made of them.

The four joins used in cursive words

Nearly every connection in lowercase cursive falls into one of these four groups. Learn to recognize them and you can work out any word, even one you have never practised.

1. Undercurve joins

This is the default join. The first letter ends at the baseline with a curve that swings up and to the right, and the next letter begins with that same upward swing. Letters that start with an undercurve include i, u, w, t, e, l, b, h, k, f, r, s, j and p. Pairs to practise: il, ut, ee, lt, ir. The join is a single smooth scoop; there should be no corner at the bottom.

2. Downcurve joins

The oval letters, a, c, d, g, o and q, begin at the top of their body and curve back to the left. To reach that starting point, the previous letter's undercurve rises all the way to the midline, then the pen curls back over the top and down into the oval. Beginners often close the oval from the bottom instead, which leaves a gap at the top of the a or a hook on the d. Pairs to practise: ea, ic, ld, ug, to.

3. Overcurve joins

Letters in the n family, n, m, v, x, y and z, begin with a hump: an overcurve that rises and turns down, like the top of an arch. The previous letter's exit stroke flows straight up into that arch. The mistake here is a flat joining line followed by a separate hump, which makes an n look like a u. Pairs to practise: in, am, ev, ly, ex.

4. Bridge joins after b, o, v and w

Four lowercase letters, b, o, v and w, do not finish at the baseline. They finish up at the midline with a short sideways stroke, often called a bridge or sidestroke. Whatever comes next has to start from that height. Because there is no undercurve to climb, the next letter is formed a little differently:

  • Bridge into e (be, oe, ve, we): the bridge dips slightly and loops straight into a small e. Keep the e loop narrow or it will crowd the previous letter.
  • Bridge into r or s (or, os, br, ws): the bridge runs directly into the top of the r shoulder or the s point without dropping to the baseline first.
  • Bridge into an oval letter (oa, bo, wo): the bridge travels over to the right and curls back into the oval from the top, just as a downcurve join does.
  • Bridge into the n family (on, om, vy): the bridge rolls straight into the hump.

Bridge joins are where most cursive words fall apart, so they deserve their own practice line. A sheet of on, or, oe, be, we, vo repeated five times will fix more of your handwriting than a page of the whole alphabet.

Quick test: write the words brown and over. Both contain two bridge joins. If the letter after each bridge sits at the same height as the rest of the word, your bridges are working.

When to lift the pen in cursive words

Cursive is called a continuous hand, but it is not written in one unbroken line from the first word to the last. A few marks are always added after the word is finished:

  • Dots on i and j. Write the whole word first, then go back and dot. Stopping to dot mid-word breaks your rhythm and usually leaves the next join crooked.
  • Crosses on t. Same rule. Finish the word, then cross each t with one short stroke. In a word with two t's, like letter, one longer crossing stroke across both is a common and perfectly readable shortcut.
  • The cross on x. The lowercase x is written as an overcurve and slant, and the crossing stroke comes after the word is complete.

Capitals are the other place where lifts happen. Capital letters that end on the right at the baseline, such as A, C, E, H, K, M, N, R and U, usually join straight into the next lowercase letter. Capitals that finish at the top or curl back to the left, such as D, F, O, P, T, V, W and X, usually do not. For those, lift the pen and start the next lowercase letter close to the capital, so the word still reads as one unit. Handwriting programs differ on a few of these, so follow the model you are practising from rather than mixing systems.

Four cursive words spelled out stroke by stroke

Here are four worked examples. Read each step, then write the word slowly with your finger on the table before you pick up a pencil. Speed comes later.

"the"

  1. t: start at the baseline with an undercurve up to just below the top line, then retrace straight down to the baseline. Finish with a small undercurve to the right.
  2. h: let that undercurve continue up into a tall loop, come straight down to the baseline, then make an overcurve hump and bring it down to the baseline again.
  3. e: from the bottom of the h, swing an undercurve up to the midline, loop back to the left and come down to the baseline with a short exit stroke.
  4. Lift the pen and cross the t.

"people"

  1. p: undercurve up to the midline, a straight downstroke well below the baseline, retrace up, then an overcurve that closes the round part at the baseline.
  2. e: undercurve from that closing point into a small e.
  3. o: undercurve up to the midline, curl back over the top and around into the oval, close it at the top, and finish with a bridge.
  4. p: from the bridge, go straight into the p's downstroke. This is the only tricky join in the word, so slow down here.
  5. l: from the bottom of the p, undercurve up into a tall loop and down.
  6. e: undercurve into the final e. No lifts needed until the end, and nothing to dot or cross.

"genesis"

  1. g: start just below the midline and curve back into the oval, then pull the stem down below the baseline and loop it back up to cross at the baseline.
  2. e, n, e: undercurve into e, then from e rise into the n's two humps (an overcurve join), then undercurve into the second e.
  3. s: undercurve up to a point just above the midline, curve down and close with a small tuck at the baseline.
  4. i: undercurve up, straight down, undercurve out.
  5. s: the final s, then lift and dot the i.

"beautiful"

  1. b: tall loop, down to the baseline, a small rounded curve back up to the midline, and a bridge.
  2. e: the bridge dips into a narrow e. This is the hardest join in the word.
  3. a: undercurve up to the midline, over the top and back into the oval (a downcurve join), then down to the baseline.
  4. u, t, i: three undercurve joins in a row. Keep the tops of u and i level and make the t a little taller.
  5. f: undercurve into a tall loop, pull the stem down below the baseline, loop back and close it at the baseline on the right.
  6. u, l: undercurve into u, then undercurve into a tall l.
  7. Lift the pen, cross the t, dot the i.

Spacing and slant: what makes cursive words readable

You can form every letter correctly and still produce a line that is hard to read. Two things decide that: the space between words and the angle of your downstrokes.

Spacing between words

Inside a word, letters touch. Between words, leave a gap about the width of a lowercase o. Children on wide-ruled paper can use two finger-widths as a guide. The gap matters more than it seems: in cursive, the exit stroke of one word points straight at the next, so a small gap makes two words look like one long one. Keep the gap consistent across the whole line. Irregular spacing is the most common reason neat handwriting still looks messy.

Watch the space inside words too. Joins should be roughly the same length. If the connecting strokes in people are twice as long as the ones in the, the eye reads it as stretched.

Keeping the slant consistent

Most taught cursive leans slightly to the right. The exact angle is not important. What matters is that every downstroke leans by the same amount. To keep it steady:

  • Tilt the paper once, about the angle of your writing arm, and do not move it for the rest of the line.
  • Move the paper up as you go down the page, rather than reaching further with your arm.
  • Check tall letters, l, h, k, b, f and t, since they show slant errors first.
  • While you are learning, draw a few light diagonal lines on your paper and write over them.

A word ladder for practising cursive words

The fastest way to improve is to practise words in order of difficulty, adding one new kind of join at a time. Work down this ladder, spending a few days on each rung, and do not move on until the words on your current rung look the same every time you write them.

Each rung is a good candidate for one practice sheet. Put five or six words from the rung on a single sheet so you practise the join, not just one word. The guide to using cursive practice sheets explains how long to trace before switching to copying.

Capital letters at the start of cursive words

Capitals begin names, places and sentences, so they come up constantly. Treat each capital in one of two ways:

  1. Joining capitals. When the capital ends at the baseline on the right, continue its final stroke straight into the next letter as an undercurve. Anna, Mary and Kate all flow this way.
  2. Non-joining capitals. When the capital ends at the top or on the left, finish it, lift, and begin the lowercase letter just to the right, close enough that the gap is smaller than a word space. Dan, Olivia, Paul and The usually work this way.

Your name is the best capital practice there is because you will write it for life. If you want a sheet built around it, the cursive name practice generator repeats your name on every line, and the sibling post on how to write your name in cursive covers first-and-last-name spacing in detail.

How to write a sentence in cursive

A sentence is just cursive words in a row, plus a capital, spacing and punctuation. Take it in this order:

  1. Plan the line. Glance at the sentence and check it will fit. Long words near the end of a line are where writing gets squashed.
  2. Write the capital, then join or lift depending on the letter, as described above.
  3. Write each word in one pass. Do not dot, cross or lift inside the word.
  4. Dot and cross after each word, before you start the next. Leaving them all to the end of the sentence is how i's go undotted.
  5. Leave an o-width gap, then start the next word with its entry stroke.
  6. Add punctuation last. Periods and commas sit on the baseline right after the final letter's exit stroke. They are not joined.

A good first sentence uses mostly joins you already know. Try The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog once you have reached rung five of the ladder, since it covers every lowercase letter, including three bridges in brown and over. Print it on a sheet, write it once a day for a week, and compare the first attempt with the last.

Conclusion

Writing good cursive words comes down to four joins, a handful of pen lifts, and steady spacing and slant. Learn the undercurve, downcurve, overcurve and bridge patterns, save the dots and crosses for the end of each word, and climb the word ladder one rung at a time. Print a sheet for the rung you are on, write for ten to fifteen minutes a day, and within a few weeks your letters will stop looking like separate letters and start reading as words.
