So, what is cursive? Cursive is joined-up handwriting: the letters of a word connect in one flowing line, so you can write with far fewer pen lifts than when you print each letter separately. Most cursive also leans slightly to the right and uses loops and connecting strokes that carry the pen from the end of one letter into the start of the next.

That short definition covers everyday school cursive, but the word has a longer story. It names a whole family of handwriting styles going back to ancient Rome, it has a specific meaning in typography, and today it also describes a kind of copy-and-paste text you see in social media bios. This guide walks through each of those meanings in turn.

What is cursive? A clear definition of cursive writing

A practical cursive writing definition has three parts:

  • Joined letters. Letters within a word are linked by connecting strokes, called joins or ligatures. Some letters, such as a capital that ends away from the baseline, may stand alone, but most of the word is written in one movement.
  • Fewer pen lifts. Because the pen stays on the paper, writing is continuous. You typically lift only between words, and to dot an i, cross a t or finish an x afterward.
  • Letterforms shaped for speed. Cursive letters are built so that each one ends where the next can begin. That is why a cursive r or s looks so different from its printed version.

People also search for "what is a cursive," usually meaning a single cursive letter or a cursive style. In that sense "a cursive" is simply one member of the family: Spencerian is a cursive, the Palmer Method is a cursive, and so on. Signatures are a good everyday example. Even people who print everything else often sign their name in a joined, personal version of cursive, because the continuous movement is fast and hard to copy exactly.

Slant and loops are common but not required. Some modern school styles are nearly upright and drop most loops, yet they still count as cursive because the letters join. The join is the defining feature, not the decoration.

Cursive meaning and etymology: where the word comes from

The English word comes from Medieval Latin cursivus, built on the Latin verb currere, "to run." The same root gives us "current," "course" and "cursor." The cursive meaning is therefore "running writing": script in which the pen runs along the line instead of stopping at every letter.

That origin is a useful clue. Cursive was never primarily about beauty. It was about getting words down quickly, whether by a Roman clerk, a Renaissance secretary or a 19th-century bookkeeper. Elegant styles developed along the way, but speed and practicality drove the form.

The history of cursive writing

Scholars disagree on many details and dates, so treat the outline below as the broad picture rather than a precise timeline.

Roman cursive

Romans wrote formal inscriptions in the capitals you still see on monuments, but everyday writing on wax tablets, wood and papyrus was much quicker and looser. Historians usually describe an older and a later form of Roman cursive. Surviving letters and notes, such as the wooden tablets found at Vindolanda near Hadrian's Wall, show ordinary people writing fast, joined, often hard-to-read script for accounts and personal messages.

Chancery hand and italic

In Renaissance Italy, scribes developed a humanist cursive that was clearer than older Gothic hands. A refined version, often called the chancery hand, was used for official documents, and writing masters published copybooks teaching it. Early printers modeled a slanted typeface on this handwriting, which is the origin of what we now call italic type.

Copperplate and roundhand

From roughly the 17th and 18th centuries, English writing masters popularized roundhand, a style written with a flexible pointed pen that produces thick downstrokes and hairline upstrokes. Because its models were printed from engraved copper plates, it is also known as copperplate. It became a standard business and correspondence hand in much of the English-speaking world.

Spencerian, Palmer, Zaner-Bloser and D'Nealian

American schooling produced its own sequence of cursive systems:

  • Spencerian script, developed by Platt Rogers Spencer in the 19th century, was an ornate, graceful business hand used widely before typewriters.
  • The Palmer Method, promoted by Austin Palmer from around the turn of the 20th century, simplified letters and emphasized arm movement for speed. It dominated many American classrooms for decades.
  • Zaner-Bloser, named for Charles Paxton Zaner and Elmer Ward Bloser, became one of the most widely used school handwriting programs, with a fairly upright, simplified cursive.
  • D'Nealian, introduced in the 1970s by Donald Thurber, starts children on slanted print letters with small tails, meant to make the later move to cursive easier.

Each system changed letter shapes a little, and publishers revised their models over time, which is why adults taught in different decades or states may form a capital Q or lowercase z differently.

Cursive writing in other scripts

Asking what is cursive in English leads to looped Latin letters, but joined handwriting exists in many scripts:

  • Cyrillic. Handwritten Russian and many related languages are typically written in cursive, and several cursive letters look quite different from their printed forms. The handwritten lowercase т, for instance, resembles a Latin m, which regularly surprises learners.
  • Arabic. Arabic script is joined by nature, even in print: most letters change shape depending on whether they connect to the letter before, after, or both.
  • Hebrew. Everyday Hebrew handwriting uses a cursive form that differs noticeably from the square letters used in print.
  • Chinese and Japanese. East Asian calligraphy has running and cursive styles in which strokes flow together and characters are abbreviated.

The common thread is the same everywhere: when people write by hand quickly, letters and strokes start to connect. Cursive is less a single alphabet than a natural result of writing at speed, which is why so many unrelated scripts arrived at joined forms independently.

Why schools dropped cursive, and why some brought cursive back

For most of the 20th century, American children learned print first and then switched to cursive in around the second or third grade. That began to change as keyboards took over schoolwork and testing.

The case for dropping it

Critics argued that classroom time was better spent on typing and other skills, that adults rarely write long passages by hand, and that print is easier to read. When the Common Core standards, adopted by many states in the 2010s, did not include cursive, a lot of schools stopped teaching it or made it optional.

The case for bringing it back

Supporters pointed out that people who cannot read cursive struggle with family letters, historical documents and handwritten notes. Some educators also argue that joined handwriting helps certain children with fluency and letter reversals, though researchers still debate how strong those effects are. A number of US states have since passed laws requiring cursive instruction again, and requirements vary from state to state.

What is cursive in typography and digital text?

Outside handwriting, the word shows up in two technical places.

Cursive fonts

In type design, "cursive" or "script" describes fonts that imitate joined handwriting. Web designers may also know that CSS has a generic font family named cursive, which asks the browser for some handwriting-style font without naming one. The result differs by device. Unicode cursive text

The cursive text you paste into an Instagram bio or a Discord name is not a font at all. It is made of characters from Unicode's Mathematical Alphanumeric Symbols block, including the Mathematical Script and Bold Script letters, plus a few older script letters such as ℬ and ℰ from the Letterlike Symbols block.

So what is cursive, in one sentence?

If you need a single answer to what is cursive, it is handwriting in which letters are joined so the pen runs across the page with few lifts. Everything else, from Roman wax tablets to copperplate, Palmer drills and copy-and-paste script letters, is a variation on that idea: writing that flows.
